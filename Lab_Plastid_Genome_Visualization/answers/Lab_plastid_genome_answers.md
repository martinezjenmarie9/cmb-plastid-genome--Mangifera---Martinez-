### 3. Can you identify the LSC, SSC, IRa, and IRb regions?

Yes. The map shows the typical four-part plastid genome organization:

**LSC (Large Single Copy):** The large region occupying most of the upper portion of the map.

**SSC (Small Single Copy):** The smaller region located at the lower portion of the map.

**IRa (Inverted Repeat A):** The inverted-repeat region on the right side of the inner circle.

**IRb (Inverted Repeat B):** The corresponding inverted-repeat region on the left side.

Together, they form the typical **LSC–IRb–SSC–IRa** circular arrangement.

### 6. Give at least one example of a gene that occurs within an inverted repeat region. Is the gene shown more than once because of the duplicated IR regions?

An example is **rpl2**, which is located in the inverted-repeat region.

Genes that are located within the IR regions can occur twice because IRa and IRb contain duplicated DNA sequences. Therefore, the same gene can appear once in IRa and once in IRb.

### 8. What does the direction of the gene arrows or gene orientation tell you about transcription?

The arrows show the direction in which the genes are oriented and transcribed. Genes pointing in one direction are transcribed from one DNA strand, while genes pointing in the opposite direction are transcribed from the other strand. Thus, the arrows help show the orientation of genes on the circular plastid genome.

### 9. Look at the GC content graph. Does GC content appear uniform?

The GC-content track appears to show some variation around the plastid genome rather than being perfectly uniform. Some regions have slightly higher or lower GC content than others.
However, the variation does not appear to represent a dramatic difference across the whole genome. It is better to describe it as regional variation in GC content rather than assigning a biological cause based only on the map.
This is consistent with my Galaxy result, which gives an overall GC content of 37.89%.

### 10. Why is a graphical plastid genome map useful?

A graphical map makes the genome much easier to understand than looking at raw DNA sequence or a long annotation list. It allows you to quickly see:

**Where genes are located**

**Which genes are in the LSC, SSC, or IR regions**

**Gene orientation**

**The distribution of different gene types**

**The positions of the duplicated IR regions**

**The overall circular organization of the plastid genome**

**Regions with different sequence characteristics, such as GC-content variation**
